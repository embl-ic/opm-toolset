Aliasing Resampling

Scan the sample with a coarser step - fewer planes, a faster volume - and rebuild the raw volume onto the finer scan grid afterwards, using the tilt of the OPM's optical spectrum. This chapter explains when that works, how to test a step on your own microscope before relying on it, and what each command does.

Experimental. The reconstruction is an adaptation of McFadden, Manton and Fiolka, Increasing the acquisition speed in oblique plane microscopy via aliasing (preprint, 2024; authors' code), not a port of the authors' pipeline. A step that works on one microscope, wavelength and sample is not certified for another: test it against a dense acquisition first, as described below.

What it does

An OPM images a tilted plane, so its optical transfer function - the range of spatial frequencies the microscope passes - is tilted too. Scanning the sample in steps repeats that spectrum at regular offsets. With a fine step the copies are far apart; with a coarse one they move closer, and interpolating between planes mixes them up. But because the spectrum is tilted, there is room between the copies: up to a point, a coarse scan still holds every copy separately, and the original can be picked out again.

The reconstruction inserts empty planes to make a fine scan grid, Fourier transforms it, keeps only the band that holds the original spectrum, and transforms back. Inside the limit this restores what a fine scan would have recorded, and it does much better than linear interpolation between planes. Beyond the limit the copies overlap and no method can separate them.

It does not recover detail the optics never passed, detail the camera's pixels could not sample, or detail blurred away by a scan that moves during the exposure. It is not a general way to fill in missing planes.

The commands

CommandInputResult
Reconstruct Raw Volume…One raw volume acquired with a coarse step: 8, 16 or 32-bit, one channel, one time point.A 32-bit raw volume on the scan grid factor times finer, and optionally its deskewed view.
Create Raw Slice Subset…A dense raw volume.Every Nth plane from a chosen offset, with the calibration and origin kept physical - a coarse scan made from a fine one.
Test Dense Raw Volume…A dense raw volume.For every factor from 2 to a maximum and every offset: the error of the reconstruction and of linear interpolation against the planes that were removed, as a Results table.
Inspect Raw Bead PSF Spectrum…A tight, isolated bead from a dense raw volume.The spectrum section at kX = 0, and the largest factor its support allows at a chosen threshold, in the Log.
Acquisition Planner…Numbers you have measured.The largest step a measured axial OTF support allows, the plane count, the volume time and the blur of a continuously moving scan.
Z-Step Advisor…Optical/acquisition parameters, optionally a static dense raw volume. Persistent metadata-selectable settings, conditional step and timing estimates, all-phase reference-agreement tests, comparison previews, and CSV/JSON records.

Image-processing commands use an open image; the advisor selects its reference explicitly and can estimate a conditional range without an image. The reconstruction holds its whole output in memory, so start with a representative region: open part of a large TIFF with the OPM Data Viewer, or crop. Split channels and time points first. The source data are never changed.

Z-Step Advisor: choosing and testing a scan step

Z-Step Advisor… opens a nonblocking dialog with the toolset's usual folding sections. The estimate, action buttons and Stop stay visible above the folds. Its settings are saved separately from Deskew and Live Processing. Close saves the last valid settings; an unfinished numeric edit does not replace a valid saved value. Reset advisor defaults affects this dialog.

  1. Select an open, static raw reference, or use None for model estimates alone. Use one channel and one time point, before deskew or deconvolution. Set the bounded ROI explicitly; all ROI coordinates are zero based. A new source gets a box centred in the left camera half when the frame is wide enough to hold two, so it does not straddle both channels and the gap between them. That assumes a dual-view camera: for a single view, draw an ROI on the structure instead. Use current ROI copies the selected image's XY rectangle and centers the requested scan depth around its current plane. Several beads in the box give a far steadier score than one.
  2. Select ExperimentalParameter(s).txt, use its recent-file dropdown, or Browse. Both ExperimentalParameters.txt and ExperimentalParameter.txt are recognized. Valid present fields overwrite the editors. Missing fields keep their numeric values but lose acquisition-specific verification. The Log lists the path and loaded fields. A file's slit width in mm is never interpreted as dimensionless NA. On opening, the sidecar of the open image wins over the file used last time; the saved file, with your edits to it, is reopened only when it is that same sidecar or there is none.
  3. Verify the acquired reference step, pixel size and angle for that original stack. A plane count mismatch leaves the association unverified; a cropped or subset stack needs its actual geometry. Confirm that the source is unchanged raw data. A matching saved path alone is not evidence that pixels or calibration are unchanged.
  4. Choose the optical evidence: illustrative NA/sheet model, approximate effective Gaussian widths, or entered OTF support. Defaults (NA 0.8, emission 520 nm, refractive index 1.333, sheet-normal FWHM 1 µm) are plausible starting values, not measurements of the setup. Measured support must apply to the channel and geometry being planned.
  5. Choose the candidate range and exposure policy. Update estimate shows conditional motion curves and a table with plane counts, actual covered span, exposure, frame period, volume time and speedup. The 0.05/0.10 OTF-threshold comparison is model sensitivity, not a statistical confidence interval.
  6. For reference testing, enter a verified offset/noise estimate, or select a representative background ROI in that reference image and use Estimate from selected image's background ROI. The estimate uses its current plane, median background and Gaussian-consistent MAD. Shot noise, spatially varying background and non-Gaussian camera noise may require a better estimate. A declared noise-free synthetic reference bypasses the noise threshold.
  7. Press Test reference ROI. Select a row in the resulting table and use Preview selected step to inspect reference/linear/Fourier raw panels, a camera-Y/scan section, and signed errors. Inserted reference display planes are linearly interpolated; they are not ground truth. Error views use NaN at inserted planes and retain signed errors.

The empirical runner tests only D = m*d0, at every phase from 0 to m−1. Its reconstruction factor is M = m*r, where r refines the output grid to d0/r; M must be at most 64; it changes only the output grid, adds no measured sample, and can shift the scores slightly through the FFT grid, mask discretisation and padding. The comparison domain intersects all retained endpoint spans and applies a common guard. Held-out and acquired samples are scored separately at original dense-plane locations. The table pools squared error and background-subtracted reference energy; it also reports the worst phase. The reference control has no held-out score.

A step is accepted only on its raw held-out error. A tested step passes when its raw held-out NRMSE is within the ceiling (10% by default) pooled and in every phase. The verdict uses that row alone: no factor passes on another's evidence, and none passes because a coarser one did no worse. The reference is a noisy measurement, and its noise and the noise the reconstruction carries from the acquired planes are independent of the structural error, so they only add to the raw error. A pass is therefore conservative: in expectation the structural error is no larger than the raw one. For a declared noise-free synthetic reference, the two are the same.

The price of that guarantee is noise. Against a noisy reference the raw held-out NRMSE cannot fall below about √(2v/P), where v is the noise variance and P the signal power, whatever the step. On the EMBL bead acquisition it sits near 0.25 at 2× and 3×, so the default ceiling accepts no step there. Raising the ceiling is a legitimate choice, as long as the accepted bound is read as that weak. A brighter or averaged reference lowers the floor. Two dense acquisitions of the same field, taken back to back, would measure the floor directly.

Noise-corrected estimates are reported beside the verdict, never used for it. The advisor fits v = σ² + gain·signal to the reference's own noise-only band, the scan frequencies outside the slab the entered optics allow. It then removes the expected floor from each held-out error to give an excess NRMSE. The Fourier floor is propagated through the reconstruction's own kernel, which is nonlocal: a voxel beside a noisier region inherits that region's noise. The signal mask is taken from a smoothed reference, so it does not favour voxels whose noise happens to be high. The fit is used only when it agrees, within 25%, with the verified background sigma, and with a second fit halfway to the band's edge. If the band holds signal, as it does when the entered optics underestimate the support, the fit mistakes that signal for noise and would subtract the very error it should measure; it is then refused. The table shows the excess, its value under the second fit, its worst phase, and the increase over factor 2, √(excess(m)² − excess(2)²). That is an increase in estimated error energy, not all the error a step adds: a cross term can hide different artefacts of equal energy. On the bead acquisition the excess is 0.14 at 2× and rises by 3.0% at 3× and 10.7% at 4×. That is the same split as matched bead widths: axial width within about 3% at 3×, and 6–11% wider at 4×. But the excess at 2× is not itself qualified. Synthetic beads of known structure leave it about 0.03 too high, and part of 0.14 may be real error. When the excess falls as the step coarsens, factor 2 itself disagrees with the reference and no increase is interpreted.

The fixed signal mask exceeds both the chosen fraction of the smoothed reference's 99.5th-percentile residual and the chosen noise-sigma threshold (defaults 10% and 5 sigma). Unknown geometry, unverified noise/background, inadequate foreground, missing phases or saturated source pixels prevent a qualified passing range. Scores may still be inspected. A passing range starts at factor 2, since m = 1 is the reference itself, and stops at the first failed or missing candidate. Isolated passes are listed separately. When factor 2 is not tested, passing points are listed without a range. No untested intermediate spacing or hardware-quantized step inherits a measured pass.

Reference agreement is not an optical-resolution measurement. This version does not automatically extract measured 3D PSFs or fit matched bead widths in XYZ. Enter externally measured support if available. The model uses the full projected Gaussian support, including the tilted sheet's cross term, and keeps the required support fixed throughout the sweep. Three effective widths describe an approximate axis-aligned Gaussian, not a measured full OTF. The intrinsic NA/sheet model does not simulate relay clipping, detector integration, finite bead size or a vectorial PSF. Deconvolution is not applied to the comparison.

For continuous motion the blur length is L = D*t/T and the motion transfer is a sinc along the scan direction. The baseline period T0 is estimated from baseline exposure plus overhead, or entered separately. Candidate frame period stays fixed unless Keep scan velocity fixed is selected. Preserving baseline blur changes exposure while retaining that period policy: doubling D at fixed T requires halving exposure. Fewer frames at fixed velocity do not halve travel time. Absolute transfer and additional loss relative to baseline are separate; a ratio near a baseline transfer zero is unavailable. Unknown/rolling-shutter/nonuniform timing cannot establish a motion-safe setting. Sufficient photon collection still needs an actual signal target and camera/photon calibration.

Export CSV + JSON writes individual phase scores, a -factors.csv with each factor's pooled and worst-phase scores, verdict and estimates, and a manifest. The manifest holds the acceptance criterion, the estimator and its noise fit, the immutable ROI pixel hash, parent dimensions, numeric settings, metadata fingerprint, provenance, common domain, mask definition, actual padding/FFT sizes, backend, completion status and conditional predictions. Export location is chosen explicitly. Physical padding minima are converted to the core's shared padding integer; actual FFT rounding is recorded. CPU is the default. CLIJ2-FFT remains experimental and should be used with other GPU operations idle; concurrent shared-context safety is not certified.

Stop and Close cancel only the advisor's registered operation; cleanup finishes on its worker. Escape and Fiji quit retain their toolset-wide meaning. Editing parameters during a run invalidates its result as a current recommendation. No source pixels, acquisition settings or Fiji installation are modified by this command.

Testing a step before you use it

  1. Acquire a dense bead volume at your usual step, at the wavelength and depth you will image.
  2. Crop one isolated bead in the raw volume and run Inspect Raw Bead PSF Spectrum…. Repeat for several beads across the field; the smallest bound is the one that counts.
  3. On a larger raw region - beads, or a sample like yours - run Test Dense Raw Volume…. It removes planes, rebuilds them and compares, at every offset. Look for the factor beyond which the Fourier error rises steeply, and check that it beats the linear error well before that.
  4. Deskew a few reconstructions (Reconstruct Raw Volume… on a subset made with Create Raw Slice Subset…, Also show deskewed volume) and compare bead widths with the dense volume, both brought onto the same fine scan grid first (see Choosing the target step). A smooth result is not proof: when copies start to overlap they can cancel, and the bead simply comes out wider.
  5. Enter what you measured in the Acquisition Planner… to see the volume time and the motion blur of the step you chose.
  6. Only then acquire with the coarser step, and keep one dense volume per session as a check.

Removing planes digitally keeps the dense scan's own exposure. A real coarse scan with a continuously moving galvo moves further during each exposure, which blurs along the scan. That cannot be tested on a dense volume; see Acquisition.

Reconstruction settings

  • Camera pixel at object: the camera pixel size at the specimen, in µm.
  • Acquired scan step: the step between the planes in the input, as acquired - not their perpendicular spacing and not the voxel size you want. A volume acquired at 0.795 µm and rebuilt to 0.265 µm is step 0.795, factor 3.
  • OPM angle: the same convention as Deskew Image.
  • Factor: the integer acquired step ÷ reconstructed step.
  • Camera background: subtracted before the reconstruction and added back after. Use one measured value for the whole volume; never normalise planes one by one.
  • Padding: planes of background added on each side, 32 by default. Fourier filtering still disturbs the edges: leave a margin out of any measurement.
  • Cosine edge: 0 keeps the band's hard edge, as in the paper. A fraction above 0 softens the edge, which reduces ringing and costs some bandwidth.
  • FFT backend: the CPU by default. The GPU backend (CLIJ2-FFT, experimental) transforms up to 16 sections at once and needs CLIJ2 with a working OpenCL device; a failure is reported, never quietly done on the CPU instead.

The output spans the first to the last acquired plane, (N-1)×factor+1 planes; no planes are invented past the ends. It is still a raw, oblique volume, calibrated at the reconstructed step. Deskew it exactly once, at that step: Also show deskewed volume does this, onto an isotropic grid at the camera pixel size. Values below the background (ringing) are kept, in 32-bit. The image's Info records every setting and the backend. Escape stops a reconstruction between sections.

Choosing the target step

The deskew interpolates linearly along the scan axis, and at a 0.265 µm step that axis is coarse for fine lateral detail along Y. A bead of 0.45 µm FWHM, computed exactly, deskews to 0.51 µm from a 0.265 µm grid, 0.48 µm from 0.1325 µm and 0.46 µm from 0.066 µm - in a dense scan and in a reconstruction alike. So when the deskewed volume is what you measure, pick a factor that gives a fine target, about 0.13 µm here: factor 4 for a 0.53 µm acquisition, factor 6 for 0.795 µm. A dense volume can be brought onto the same grid by reconstructing it at its own step with factor 2, which keeps a comparison between the two fair. A finer grid costs memory and time, may change noise and ringing, and creates no optical bandwidth.

Where the starting values come from

When ImageJ still knows where the image was opened from, the dialogs read the ExperimentalParameters.txt beside it: pixel size, angle, exposure, and the step the deskew itself uses (galvo step × steps per plane, else Step size /um). If that file describes a different number of planes than the image has - a subset, or a dense scan next to a sparse one - its step is not used, and the dialog says the step is unverified. A cropped or duplicated image has no file behind it; the dialog then shows clearly labelled fallbacks of 0.116 µm, 0.265 µm, 25° and 100 ms. These are starting values, not a calibration.

Geometry and the Fourier band

In the raw volume, camera row u and scan plane v sit at

Y = p·cos(θ)·u + d·v
Z = −p·sin(θ)·u + constant

with p the camera pixel at the specimen, θ the OPM angle and d the reconstructed scan step - the deskew's own geometry (see the geometry section of the Live Deskew chapter). A pattern with lab frequencies (kY, kZ) appears on the raw grid at

fu = p·(cos(θ)·kY − sin(θ)·kZ)        fv = d·kY

cycles per sample, so fv − s·fu = d·tan(θ)·kZ with s = d / (p·cos θ). The reconstruction keeps

| wrap(fv − s·fu) | < 1 / (2·factor)        wrap(x) = x − round(x)

which selects the axial band |kZ| < cot(θ) / (2·D), D the acquired step - for content whose spectrum lies in that band. On the raw grid the band repeats on the fine grid's own reciprocal lattice, so a pattern that was already aliased when the planes were recorded (kY = 1/d, kZ = cot(θ)/d samples exactly like a constant) is not told apart: the method separates the copies the coarse scan makes, not aliasing already in the data. The wrap is essential, not a detail: a frequency on the raw grid is only defined to within one cycle per sample, and at typical settings the band wraps round the scan axis for every lateral frequency above 1 / (2·d) - 1.9 cycles/µm at a 0.265 µm step. Without it, fine lateral detail along Y is removed together with its aliases. The band is applied on the raw grid before any interpolation, and is the same for every X, so each Y-scan section is filtered on its own. Factor 1 changes nothing.

The band separates the aliases; it does not denoise. Everything the camera recorded inside the axial band is kept, including the noise above the optical cut-off, and in the rebuilt planes that noise is not the noise a dense scan would have recorded there. On the EMBL bead acquisition the rebuilt planes differ more from the dense scan than the acquired ones do, even where the bead widths agree. Judge a step by bead widths and by the structures you image, not by a smooth appearance or by the error against a noisy dense volume alone.

The resulting limit on the acquired step is

D < cot(θ) / (2·Kz)

for an axial OTF half-support Kz in cycles/µm. It is sufficient for this band under this geometry, not an optimum for every OTF shape: allow a margin, and remember that the camera sampling, the noise and the threshold that defines Kz all move it. The bead inspector looks at one section (kX = 0) of the spectrum and can miss support elsewhere; use several beads, and a full 3-D OTF estimate, before settling on a step. The authors' MATLAB adjusts the band angle empirically; this implementation uses the geometric angle.

Acquisition, exposure and deconvolution

With a continuously moving scan, each exposure smears the sample along Y by L = scan speed × exposure = D × exposure / frame period. That blur multiplies the spectrum by sinc(π·kY·L), whose first zero is at 1/L; nothing removes it afterwards. A coarser step with the same frame period and exposure scans faster and blurs more. Cutting the exposure by the factor keeps the original blur length - at a 100 ms frame period and 100 ms exposure, 50 ms for factor 2 and 33 ms for factor 3 - at the cost of light: factor times fewer photons, and a signal-to-noise ratio √factor lower at best. Synchronised illumination or a step-and-hold scan avoids the blur.

Measure the frame period rather than assuming it equals the exposure; the planner takes it, or exposure + overhead when it is left at 0. A volume takes about planes × frame period + flyback. The planner reports the blur length and the first zero of its transfer function; it cannot tell you how much light is enough.

For deconvolution, measure the PSF with the same light sheet, wavelength, depth, camera settings and scan timing, and process it on the same geometry and grid as the data: reconstruct first, then deskew, then deconvolve. Check the result independently - ringing below the background is not compatible with a Poisson Richardson-Lucy model as it stands.
